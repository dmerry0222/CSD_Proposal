Proposal Draft, October 2026

Career Strategy & Design

Proposal for the Revised Shared Gen Ed Curriculum

An outcome this consequential requires an intentional curriculum.

Suffolk's academic programs prepare students to think critically, engage with complex questions, and develop expertise within and across disciplines. Career Strategy & Design builds on that foundation by ensuring every undergraduate receives structured opportunities to explore career pathways, develop professional skills and relationships, and gain meaningful work experience.

Career preparation and postgraduation opportunities are central considerations for prospective students and families. At Suffolk, the expectations are especially clear: our entering students overwhelmingly identify career advancement, relevant employment, and internship experience as priorities for their college education.

This proposed requirement establishes a common progression through two 1-credit courses, qualifying work-based learning, and a final launch checkpoint, supported by one evolving Career Plan that students author throughout their undergraduate experience.

What is the urgency?

Students invest years of their lives and substantial financial resources in college, often taking on debt that will follow them long after graduation. They trust that this investment will help them find meaningful work, achieve economic security, and have greater choice in their futures. Helping students realize that promise is part of our educational responsibility.

Suffolk faculty develop students’ career readiness through disciplinary knowledge and transferable capacities such as critical thinking, ethical judgment, collaboration, and strong written and verbal communication. Students also need explicit instruction and guided practice in career management skills, such as:

  • understanding and persuasively articulating their unique skills and experiences;
  • developing authentic relationships with trusted mentors, supervisors, and future colleagues who can share insights and open doors;
  • evaluating workforce data, insights from trusted sources, and one's lived experience to make sound and confident career decisions;
  • building a strong foundation of real work and positive regard in their intended field.

Structured and sequential development of these skills is especially important for students with limited prior access to professional social capital or industry-relevant guidance. Research shows that purposeful instruction can strengthen students’ ability to seek support and build the relationships that help them move forward.

Career opportunity is central to the promise of a Suffolk education

Students come to Suffolk expecting their education to expand their intellectual horizons and career prospects; this is a promise we explicitly make in our admissions materials. Data shows that career goals are one of, if not the most important factors in students' decision to attend college, and those statistics are even more pronounced at Suffolk.

While national data consistently associate higher educational attainment with higher earnings and lower unemployment, those results are not automatic. Strada and the Burning Glass Institute found that only about half of bachelor’s graduates secured employment in an occupation typically requiring a college degree within a year of graduation. Helping students translate their education into opportunity is an essential part of fulfilling the promise we make and they trust

A university’s ability to prepare graduates for career success has direct implications for its financial stability, eligibility for federal student loans, and credibility with prospective students and families. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act’s earnings test ties academic programs’ eligibility for federal student loans to graduates’ earnings. Undergraduate programs are measured against an earnings benchmark for working adults with only a high school education; programs that fail the test in two of three years lose access to federal Direct Loans.

These accountability requirements coincide with demographic pressures close to home: demographic research projects 8% fewer high school graduates in Massachusetts and 17% fewer across the Northeast between 2023 and 2041. Together, these pressures strengthen the case for making career preparation a visible and credible part of Suffolk’s educational promise. Prospective students and families should be able to see how every student will receive guidance, gain relevant experience, and develop the ability to pursue meaningful work after graduation.

Career management skills require explicit instruction, practice, and assessment

Suffolk’s curriculum develops and assesses capabilities foundational to students’ professional lives, including disciplinary knowledge, communication, ethical reasoning, and critical thinking. Career management builds on that foundation by teaching students how to investigate and pursue the opportunities for which their education has prepared them. NACE recognizes these abilities within its *Career + Self-Development* competency, including self-assessment, planning, navigating opportunities, and developing professional relationships.

In conversations with Suffolk faculty, students’ challenges often concern navigating the transition from college to work: being aware of possible occupations and roles, knowing when and how to prepare for a search, explaining how their skills apply to a role, or approaching professionals and sustaining relationships. These observations point to concrete educational tasks: researching careers through labor-market data, job postings, and professional conversations; assembling and communicating evidence of one’s capabilities; and translating that investigation into an actionable plan.

Explicit instruction matters because access to professional knowledge and relationships is uneven. First-generation and historically underrepresented students often have had fewer opportunities to learn how particular professions operate or develop connections within them. Research documents disparities in internship participation and shows that purposeful teaching can strengthen students’ networks. Students from any background, however, can miss optional guidance when they do not recognize its relevance, know when to seek it, or have time among competing responsibilities. These are capabilities students can learn, but ensuring that every student develops them requires more than waiting for individuals to seek out support.

Sustained, supervised work gives students opportunities to test their career expectations, apply their skills, respond to feedback, and build relationships through their contributions. Employers value this preparation: 81% of respondents to AAC&U’s 2025 survey were more likely to consider hiring a candidate who had completed an internship or apprenticeship. The survey also affirmed the value of leadership, research, employment, and other applied experiences.

These findings and observations inform Career Strategy & Design’s learning goals and objectives. Assignments, feedback, and successive Career Plans would assess students’ ability to investigate options, demonstrate their capabilities, build relationships, and prepare for and learn from work-based experiences. They would also help identify barriers such as competing work obligations and inadequate compensation.

Ensuring every Suffolk student acquires these skills

A university can offer excellent career services and meaningful classroom experiences without ensuring that every student receives a coherent foundation in career management. Gallup and Lumina report that 53% of alumni wished they had accessed more career coaching as students , while Strada found that only 20% of recent public four-year graduates experienced career coaching that included guidance, timely information, and support. These findings strengthen the case for making foundational career learning an expected part of every student’s education, supported by continuing access to individualized advising.

The Career Leadership Collective’s Career Ecosystem Model provides an organizing framework for this approach: a commitment to every student, shared career learning outcomes, embedded milestones, and an empowered campus community. Strada’s principles for education-to-career guidance similarly call for support that is universal, proactive, embedded, and sustained over time . Together, these frameworks support a coordinated approach in which the career center contributes expertise, resources, and connections, while faculty and advisors connect career learning to students’ academic interests and decisions. Faculty already play a substantial role: in NACE’s 2024 survey, 92% of faculty respondents reported that students in their disciplinary area had sought career advice from them during the previous year. A shared framework can give those conversations greater continuity and better information.

At Suffolk, that means a required sequence with room for different interests, disciplines, and professional goals. Early exploration should inform preparation for work-based learning; that experience should, in turn, inform students’ remaining academic choices and plans for life after graduation. A continuing Career Plan would connect these stages, giving students a place to document their thinking, evidence, relationships, and next steps. It would also give faculty, advisors, and career staff a common starting point for guidance, while allowing the university to examine patterns in students’ progress and unmet needs across populations. The educational value comes from each stage building on what students have already learned and helping them make the next consequential decision.

We should not have to wait until after graduation for our first assessment of students' ability to secure a job. Clear learning outcomes and recurring milestones would enable formative assessment students’ developing capabilities while there is still time to provide feedback, remove barriers, and connect them with support A universal requirement also creates a corresponding institutional responsibility: participation must be feasible, expectations must be clear, and students must have appropriate ways to meet them. The purpose is to make career development a visible, connected, and assessable part of a Suffolk education, with sustained support for every student’s progress.

The Career Plan

The Career Plan is an online tool currently in development, designed to live as en embedded and connected Canvas tool through LTI connections, and made specifically for Suffolk. It connects students’ learning, experiences, and decisions across their time at Suffolk. Students would use it to document the possibilities they are investigating, the evidence informing their choices, and the actions they intend to take next. As they gain experience and receive feedback, they would revisit and revise that plan. The value of guided planning is supported by the 2024 National Alumni Career Mobility report, which identifies institutional help in creating a career plan as a significant predictor of stronger alumni-reported career preparation.

The plan also provides a shared starting point for guidance across courses, academic advising, and career services, allowing each conversation to build on the student’s prior work. For students, it would make progress and next steps explicit; for faculty and advisors, it would reveal opportunities to help; and for Suffolk, aggregate information would help identify patterns in students’ goals, preparation, and unmet needs. This design puts into practice the principle that career guidance should begin early, continue throughout college, and connect across the settings where students learn and seek support.

Proof-of-Concept Examples of the Career Plan at Each of the Four Milestones

The Career Plan is a technology interface for students, faculty, and staff at Suffolk, and is currently in development. It is designed to live as an embedded and connected LTI component in Canvas, allowing for easy access for educators, connection to assignments in any Canvas class, and a pre-emptive strike against having “one more place” to do things online.

How the Career Plan Innovates Career Advising at Suffolk

Because students answer in structured fields rather than in free-standing essays, the Career Plan can do things a submitted document cannot: carry a student’s own words into their next plan, follow up on the goals they set, show patterns across a cohort, point advisors to the students who most need a conversation, and give instructors a view of what their students are working toward. CS&D Curriculum Overview

Two 1-Credit Courses: CE-100 and CE-200

The two one-credit Career Strategy & Design courses create dedicated time for students to explore career pathways, connect their academic choices to future goals, and develop the confidence to pursue them. This strengthens the value of their Suffolk education by helping students find purpose in their coursework and build meaningful connections within the University. It also reinforces Suffolk's enrollment and retention propositions: students who see a clear path forward have stronger reasons to enroll and persist. While individual career advising remains essential, teaching and assessing this work in the required core curriculum ensures that every student receives structured preparation for post-graduation success and enables faculty and career advisors to identify unmet needs and provide timely, targeted interventions.

Within the broader CSD sequence, CE-100 establishes a foundation for informed exploration early in the student journey. CE-200 builds on that foundation as students strengthen their professional readiness and begin actively pursuing opportunities. Together, the courses prepare students to secure meaningful work-based learning experiences, enter them with clearer goals, and translate what they learn into future opportunities.

The required courses also establish the foundation for Suffolk's longitudinal Career Plan, ensuring that every student meaningfully engages with it and develops an initial body of work that can evolve throughout their education. Rather than relying on voluntary participation, the curriculum creates universal points of engagement and assessment, while the Career Plan preserves that work for continued use by students, faculty, and advisors. Together, they make career development a sustained and integrated element of every student's Suffolk education.

Work-Based Learning

A substantial and growing body of research demonstrates the value of internships and other forms of work-based learning in strengthening students’ academic learning, professional development, and post-graduation career outcomes. This evidence spans research from the career education field, leading higher education organizations such as AAC&U, and studies conducted by labor-market researchers, employers, and workforce consulting organizations.

Explore the Evidence for Work-Based Learning

Five Criteria for Work-Based Learning in the SGE Core

While it is sometimes useful shorthand to refer to this as "the internship requirement," it is important to clarify that work-based learning encompasses a broader range of experiences that provide students with opportunities to apply their knowledge and skills in real-world settings. To create a consistent definition of work-based learning that distinguishes it from other (very valuable!) forms of experiential education, the SGE committee posited these five criteria, which have been reviewed and revised based on faculty feedback:

CriterionDescriptionVerification and validation
1Real Work
 A majority of the student's responsibilities consist of substantive, career-relevant work that draws upon or extends academic learning and advances career exploration or preparation. The student submits a job description and three student-authored learning goals for approval by the relevant faculty member or other designated party.
2Meaningful Time
 The student completes at least 120 hours, including required training and activities integral to the role. With prior approval, two experiences may be combined to reach 120 hours, provided each independently satisfies all other criteria. Anticipated dates and hours are approved in advance. The student and supervisor confirm completion at the end; hourly logging is not required.
3Professional Supervision
 A qualified professional or field-specific equivalent provides appropriate training, ongoing guidance and feedback, and an end-of-experience performance evaluation. Site approval confirms the supervisor's qualifications and responsibilities. The supervisor completes an evaluation administered or supported by the Career Center.
4Expanded Network
 The student engages with at least two professionals beyond existing classroom, peer, and immediate supervisory relationships, gaining exposure to perspectives, pathways, communities, or opportunities relevant to their goals. Off-campus experiences are generally expected to provide these interactions through the work itself. For on-campus roles, the student and supervisor identify a plan for at least three purposeful interactions, such as informational interviews, professional meetings, external collaborations, conferences, or site visits.
5Integrated Learning
 The student identifies what they learned, connects it to academic and career development, and communicates the experience's value in a professional context. Through an internship course or analogous academic mechanism, the student completes structured reflection, updates their resume and professional profile, and submits Career Plan v3.0, connecting the experience to prior learning, strengths, development needs, and future goals.

Courses, Credit, and Assessment

The proposal recommends that every qualifying work-based learning experience be connected to a registered academic course. This creates a consistent framework for academic oversight, assessment, and degree-audit tracking while allowing experiences to count toward major requirements where appropriate. Course registration can also support international students' work authorization processes, subject to applicable eligibility requirements.

Importantly, credit-bearing WBL courses also provide an established mechanism for recognizing and compensating the faculty responsible for supervising students' academic learning. The proposed model preserves opportunities for departments to award their customary three or four credits while accommodating other credit arrangements when appropriate.

Ensuring Financial Accessibility

Requiring course registration must not create new financial barriers to participation. Full-credit WBL courses may generate overload charges for students already carrying a full academic schedule, while summer tuition can create significant expenses that financial aid may not cover.

Suffolk will need to establish accessible options before implementing the requirement, potentially including zero-credit registration, revised tuition and registration policies, and financial aid solutions. These arrangements must be developed collaboratively with faculty, academic leadership, the Registrar, Financial Aid, and International Student Services.

Resolving these barriers is a prerequisite for implementation, not a problem to address after the requirement takes effect.

Common Requirements Across WBL Courses

Participating courses may retain additional assignments and expectations appropriate to their disciplines, but all will incorporate a small set of common requirements:

  • Career Plan v3.0: Students reflect on their contributions, document professional growth, and identify how the experience informs their remaining time at Suffolk and plans after graduation.
  • Updated professional materials: Students revise their resumes and relevant professional profiles or portfolios to reflect the experience and skills developed.
  • Career readiness assessment: Students and supervisors complete the NACE Ready competency assessment through Suitable, allowing students to compare their self-assessments with supervisor feedback. Departments may also incorporate additional questions relevant to their fields.

The shared assessment framework will help Suffolk evaluate student development across WBL pathways and identify opportunities for improvement, while allowing departments to maintain the academic expectations appropriate to their programs.

Qualifying Experiences

Below are details about four types of experiences that could satisfy the Work-Based Learning requirement, if they met the 5 criteria above and were approved by the relevant faculty or administrator:

InternshipFaculty-Led ResearchQualifying Campus EmploymentApproved Project at Current Employer
What Qualifies?

Substantive, supervised professional work that meets all five WBL criteria; an internship designation alone does not guarantee eligibility.

Meaningful participation in faculty-directed research with defined responsibilities, supervision, and opportunities for professional development.

Substantive campus positions or approved expansions of existing roles that provide meaningful professional responsibilities.

A separately defined project or expanded responsibilities beyond the student's routine employment, approved with supervisor involvement.

Special Considerations

Verify the quality of supervision, substantive responsibilities, and opportunities for learning rather than relying on the internship title.

Ensure opportunities to develop professional relationships beyond the immediate faculty supervisor.

Distinguish qualifying professional work from routine student employment; eligible positions may receive standing approval.

Establish a documented project scope and clear learning objectives that distinguish the experience from existing job duties.

Educational Value

Experience professional practice firsthand, develop workplace relationships, and explore potential employment pathways.

Participate in scholarly inquiry, contribute to research, and connect with a broader disciplinary community.

Build professional capabilities through meaningful campus work, potentially reducing financial and scheduling barriers.

Apply academic learning in an established workplace while developing new capabilities and taking on greater responsibility.

Modeling Required Work-Based Learning Placements

There are many students, and entire departments, who are already engaging in internship, research, or other experiences that would satisfy the WBL requirement, or could do so with some minor adjustments to meet the five criteria above. But by far the most common question this proposal meets with is, “But what if we can’t find experiences for every student?”

It is important to remember that students in this updated curriculum will have had two classes dedicated to planning for this experience. They will not start looking for an internship 3 months before they want to do one, as is the case for many students today. But it is important to map out what this looks like as a throughput.

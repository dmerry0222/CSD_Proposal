What the evidence says about work-based learning

National research on work-based learning and what it does to students' outcomes after graduation. The strongest causal evidence applies to paid internships and is labeled as such. Several of the largest effects hold for work-based learning of any kind.

A practice the curriculum already recognizes

Internships are not an idea arriving from outside the academy. They are one of the eleven high-impact educational practices named in George Kuh's 2008 report for the Association of American Colleges and Universities, alongside first-year seminars, undergraduate research, writing-intensive courses, and capstone projects. Four of those eleven appear somewhere in the milestone structure proposed here. The question in front of the faculty is not whether to adopt an unfamiliar practice. It is whether to extend one already on the list to every student rather than to the students who find their own way to it.

The more useful contribution from that literature is the quality standard that followed it. Kuh and O'Donnell argued in 2013 that calling a practice high-impact guarantees nothing on its own, because the benefits depend on eight conditions actually being met. An experience that fails those conditions is not a high-impact practice. It is a job.

That is the case for a defined set of criteria rather than an open requirement satisfied by any work experience. The five criteria proposed for a qualifying experience are a work-based translation of the AAC&U conditions: substantive work held to a real standard, sustained effort across a defined period, supervision and professional relationships, structured reflection, and application of academic learning in a professional setting. Faculty asked to approve this requirement are being asked to apply a quality framework most of them already accept in a setting where it has not previously been enforced.

The return on a work-based experience

Work-based learning shows up in graduates' paychecks, and it keeps showing up years later. Bachelor's degree holders who had a work-based learning experience report $13,859 more in annual personal income than peers matched on race and ethnicity, gender, age, and field of study. The matching matters: this is not a comparison of business majors with art history majors.

The most rigorous causal evidence narrows to paid internships specifically. Drawing on restricted-access federal data covering a national cohort of bachelor's completers, researchers found a 15 percent earnings advantage one year after graduation for students who had completed a paid internship. That gap does not close with time. Four years out it has widened to 16 percent.

Earnings are the easiest outcome to measure but not the most revealing one. The same study found that students with a paid internship were 14 percentage points more likely to hold a first job that actually requires their degree, and 13 points more likely to describe that job as part of a career rather than simply a job. Unpaid internships and practica produce smaller versions of the same effect, up to six points and twelve points respectively. That distinction matters for a requirement that will have to recognize more than one kind of qualifying experience.

Some of the mechanism is visible before a student's first day of work. In an audit study that sent otherwise identical résumés to employers, the versions listing an internship drew 14 percent more callbacks. The line on the page is doing work in the screening pile, independent of anything the student learned on the job.

The earnings advantage holds even after accounting for field of study, gender, and race or ethnicity. It is not a reflection of which students manage to get internships in the first place.

Strada Education Foundation, 2022; Research in Higher Education, 2026.

What changes that salary does not capture

The effects that have nothing to do with salary are, if anything, larger. Recent graduates who had work-based learning are 39 percentage points more likely to say their education helped them achieve their goals. They are 11 points more likely to agree the education was worth the cost, and they report higher career satisfaction years afterward.

Worth-the-cost is not a soft measure. It is the question that drives enrollment decisions, alumni giving, and the public argument about whether a degree pays for itself. An eleven-point swing on that item, attributable to an experience the institution can require, is a consequential finding for any university making its case to families and to the state.

Researchers found no statistically significant difference in the benefit by race, gender, or socioeconomic status. The advantage is consistent across groups. What differs sharply is who gets access to the experience.

Strada Education Foundation, 2022.

What students say the experience is worth

Students who complete these experiences rate them highly and specifically. Eighty-one percent scored their experience at least 7 out of 10 on making them a stronger candidate for the job they want, and two in five gave it a perfect 10. Three quarters report being extremely or very satisfied with an internship. Eight in ten say a supervisor gave them quite a bit or a great deal of mentorship toward their career goals, and that holds across internships, practica, project-based learning, and undergraduate research alike.

More useful than the overall ratings is what separates a valuable experience from a forgettable one, because that is what a set of criteria can actually govern. Students whose experience expanded their professional network a great deal rated its career value 9.3 out of 10. Students whose experience did not expand their network at all rated it 5.5. Skill development follows a similar pattern: 76 percent of paid interns said the experience built technical skills relevant to their goals, with practica at 81 percent, and 70 percent of interns said it built broad skills that transfer across careers.

The implication is that quality is not mysterious. Supervisor support, genuine mentorship, substantive work, and contact with people in the field are the dimensions that separate the experiences students value from the ones they do not, and those are the dimensions a qualifying-experience standard can be written around.

What employers do with the credential

AAC&U's most recent employer survey, conducted with Morning Consult in August 2025 among 1,030 employers, found that 81 percent would be more likely to consider hiring a graduate who had completed an internship or apprenticeship, tied with having held a leadership role at the top of the list. An earlier AAC&U survey of 496 executives and hiring managers reached the same conclusion in softer terms, with more than four in five saying that completing an applied learning experience would make them more likely to consider a candidate.

The most frequently cited study in this area is also the one to handle most carefully. Gallup and Purdue surveyed 29,560 bachelor's degree holders in 2014 and found that graduates who had an internship or job applying what they learned in class, worked on a project lasting a semester or more, and were actively involved in extracurricular activities had roughly double the odds of being engaged at work. Only 6 percent could say they had all three. Two cautions apply. The internship item is bundled with two others, so the study cannot isolate what the internship itself contributed, and the design is retrospective self-report rather than the matched comparison used in the earnings research above. It is the most quotable finding in the field and not the most rigorous one.

From the employer side, 62 percent of the 2024 intern class received a full-time offer from their host organization. That rate varies sharply by format: 72 percent for in-person programs against roughly 56 percent where the work mixed remote and in-person. For a requirement that will need to accept remote placements, the gap is worth tracking.

Demand far exceeds access

Students are not ambivalent about this. Ninety-six percent of those who took an internship did so to connect their education with career opportunities, either to gain experience in a specific field or to test whether that field was a good fit. Seventy percent of first-year students arrive planning to complete one.

Far fewer manage it. Fewer than one in four secure a paid internship and fewer than half complete an internship of any kind. The most careful participation measure available, taken across 12,130 students at seventeen institutions, put the rate at 21.5 percent, well below the 50 to 60 percent long assumed from older and looser estimates. Institutions have generally been working from a number roughly twice the real one.

The gap is not a motivation problem. Six in ten students at four-year institutions who did not intern wanted to but could not, and 55 percent of non-interns report strong interest in doing so. Interest is not the binding constraint, which means interventions aimed at persuading students to care will not move the number.

The obstacles students name are consistent across studies and they are structural: a heavy course load is the single most-cited barrier, followed by not knowing how to find an internship, too few opportunities in their field, and the need to hold a paying job.

Strada Education Foundation, 2024; National Survey of College Internships, 2021.

Each of those four barriers is institutional rather than personal. Course load is a curricular design question. Not knowing how to find an opportunity is a preparation question. Thin opportunity supply in a given field is an employer development question. The need to earn is a question of whether the experience is paid or carries credit. None of them is addressed by encouragement, and all of them are addressed by structure.

What the evidence does and does not settle

Three claims here are well supported. Paid internships raise early-career earnings and improve the quality of the first job, and those effects survive careful matching on student characteristics. Work-based learning of any kind improves how graduates assess the value of their education, by margins large enough to matter institutionally. And the benefits appear to be distributed evenly across demographic groups, while access to the experiences is not.

Two things the evidence does not settle are worth stating plainly. The causal estimates are strongest for paid internships, so claims about unpaid or for-credit experiences should be made more cautiously and at the smaller effect sizes the research actually reports. And none of this research tests a required experience, because no national dataset describes a population where participation was universal. The findings describe what happens to students who complete these experiences, under conditions where roughly one in five does.

A third caution belongs here because a faculty member who follows this literature will raise it. Kilgo, Sheets, and Pascarella, working with longitudinal data from the Wabash National Study, found the measured effects of high-impact practices uneven across practices and across outcomes rather than uniformly strong. The designation is not a guarantee, which is the same point Kuh and O'Donnell made about quality conditions. The case for this requirement does not rest on high-impact status. It rests on matched-design evidence about earnings and employment that the high-impact literature does not itself contain, and the two should not be conflated when the proposal is defended.

What the evidence establishes well enough to act on is the shape of the problem: students want the experience, the experience works, and most students do not get it for reasons the institution controls.
